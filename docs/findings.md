# Cross-Venue Prediction Market Microstructure: Findings from NBA Finals 2026

*Aditya N. Bhosale — May 2026*

This work began as a search problem and ended as a structural one. The intent was to find prediction markets cross-listed on Kalshi and Polymarket with enough bilateral liquidity to support cross-venue analysis. The search returned exactly one category — 2026 NBA Finals team-win markets — across four candidate domains (sports, macro/Fed, politics, crypto). Three of those NBA Finals markets (OKC, CLE, NYK) had combined open interest near $110M at curation time, enough to make their microstructure worth examining in detail. The asymmetry of the discovery itself is the first finding: the cross-venue prediction-market universe is much narrower than the per-venue universes suggest, and that narrowness is structural rather than incidental. Kalshi lists CFTC-regulated event contracts that have no Polymarket counterpart; Polymarket lists cultural and tail-event markets that Kalshi avoids. Where the universes do overlap, they overlap thinly.

The headline empirical finding sits in the OKC market. Across four timestamped snapshots spanning roughly twenty-two hours, the paper mid-price discrepancy between Kalshi and Polymarket on the OKC YES contract decayed from 1.00¢ to 0.50¢ to 0.00¢, and then held at 0.00¢ across a confirming snapshot. No executable arbitrage ever fired — the paper edge sat below the conservative fee threshold (~3¢ all-in on a 50¢ contract: $0.02/contract Kalshi execution plus 2% Polymarket taker) at every observed point. Yet the two venues equilibrated to identical mids without any incentive that the standard arbitrage model can name. The most natural reading is that market makers operating on one venue are paying attention to the other, and absorbing small dislocations as a matter of pricing hygiene rather than profit capture. This is convergence by attention, not by mechanism, and it is exactly the kind of pattern that becomes invisible if you only look at executed trades.

The NYK and CLE markets surfaced a different kind of cross-venue signal. Both began the observation window with active Polymarket books — NYK with 132 bid levels and 97 ask levels at $16M open interest, CLE with deep liquidity at the long tail (book centered around 0.4% implied probability). Within roughly twenty-four hours, both Polymarket books returned 404 from the CLOB; the Kalshi books on the same markets remained active throughout. Liquidity withdrawal of this kind — same event, same resolution criterion, one venue continues quoting and the other stops — is not visible from price data alone. It is a venue-level decision that propagates instantly to anyone trying to execute across both books, and it propagates silently. A cross-venue terminal would have surfaced the 404 as an event the moment it happened; the per-venue interfaces show only an absent listing, which can be misread as a market that never existed.

The three markets together exhibit three distinct microstructure regimes despite resolving on the same event series. OKC is the clean case: similar mids on both venues, similar spreads (~210 bps), depth on both sides, and the convergence behavior described above. NYK is the asymmetric case: Kalshi's spread is roughly twenty times wider than Polymarket's (769 bps vs 39 bps) while the markets are priced consistently. CLE is the extreme-probability case: Polymarket trades the long tail down to 0.4%, while Kalshi's YES book has zero bidders at any price because nobody is willing to write a contract at that probability on a per-contract-fee venue. The same event yields three different liquidity shapes, and the regime is determined by where the implied probability sits in the venues' respective fee and incentive structures.

The framing that ties these findings together is that the cross-venue value sits in observability rather than arbitrage capture. Every executable arb computation in this study returned zero — paper discrepancies exist but do not cross the realistic fee threshold. The convergence pattern, the liquidity withdrawal, and the regime differentiation are all real, but none of them produce a trade. They produce information about how the prediction-market structure actually behaves, and that information is what a real-time cross-venue terminal exists to surface. The arb is a red herring; the structure is the product.
