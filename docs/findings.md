# Cross-Venue Prediction Market Microstructure: Findings from NBA Finals 2026

*Aditya N. Bhosale — May 2026*

This work began as a search problem and ended as a structural one. The intent was to find prediction markets cross-listed on Kalshi and Polymarket with enough bilateral liquidity to support cross-venue analysis. The search returned exactly one category — 2026 NBA Finals team-win markets — across four candidate domains (sports, macro/Fed, politics, crypto). Three of those NBA Finals markets (OKC, CLE, NYK) had combined open interest near $110M at curation time, enough to make their microstructure worth examining in detail. The asymmetry of the discovery itself is the first finding: the cross-venue prediction-market universe is much narrower than the per-venue universes suggest, and that narrowness is structural rather than incidental. Kalshi lists CFTC-regulated event contracts that have no Polymarket counterpart; Polymarket lists cultural and tail-event markets that Kalshi avoids. Where the universes do overlap, they overlap thinly.

The headline empirical finding sits in the OKC market. Across four timestamped snapshots spanning roughly twenty-two hours, the paper mid-price discrepancy between Kalshi and Polymarket on the OKC YES contract decayed from 1.00¢ to 0.50¢ to 0.00¢, and then held at 0.00¢ across a confirming snapshot. No executable arbitrage ever fired — the paper edge sat below the conservative fee threshold (~3¢ all-in on a 50¢ contract: $0.02/contract Kalshi execution plus 2% Polymarket taker) at every observed point. Yet the two venues equilibrated to identical mids without any incentive that the standard arbitrage model can name. The most natural reading is that market makers operating on one venue are paying attention to the other, and absorbing small dislocations as a matter of pricing hygiene rather than profit capture. This is convergence by attention, not by mechanism, and it is exactly the kind of pattern that becomes invisible if you only look at executed trades.

The Polymarket side of the dataset revealed a three-stage market lifecycle that Kalshi does not appear to share. Active markets with substantial remaining uncertainty (the OKC contract throughout the observation window) remain fully quoted on both sides at meaningful prices. Markets where the outcome has become effectively certain but the contract has not yet settled — the NYK YES contract after the team's elimination — enter what we describe as "lighthouse mode": the book remains structurally active with many price levels, but quotes have collapsed to the extreme tails (best_bid 0.001, best_ask 0.999). Tail-probability outcomes that were never expected to resolve YES (CLE YES at ~0.4% probability) are fully delisted: the CLOB returns "No orderbook exists" on a structurally valid token ID. Kalshi behaves differently — it maintains active books with multi-million-contract boundary stubs at $0.01 on both sides of an effectively-resolved market, rather than delisting. The convention difference is itself a finding: two venues with overlapping inventory exhibit different defaults for what to do with markets that are decided but not yet paid out.

The three markets together exhibit three distinct microstructure regimes despite resolving on the same event series. OKC is the clean case: similar mids on both venues, similar spreads, depth on both sides, and the convergence behavior described above. NYK is the post-elimination lighthouse case: both venues have collapsed to boundary liquidity at the extremes — Polymarket quoting 0.001/0.999 with a structurally active book of 125+ bids and 90+ asks, Kalshi maintaining multi-million-contract bids at $0.01 on both YES and NO sides. The simple midpoint computation reports both venues at $0.50, which is degenerate by construction; the structural finding is that the two venues independently arrive at the same response to a resolved-but-unsettled market. CLE is the extreme-probability case: Polymarket has fully delisted YES, while Kalshi maintains a one-sided book with no YES bids at any price and a deep NO bid stack pricing CLE at ~99.6% NO. The same event yields three different liquidity shapes, and the regime is determined by how close to resolution each market sits.

The framing that ties these findings together is that the cross-venue value sits in observability rather than arbitrage capture. Every executable arb computation in this study returned zero — paper discrepancies exist but do not cross the realistic fee threshold. The convergence pattern, the lifecycle differences between venues, and the regime differentiation are all real, but none of them produce a trade. They produce information about how the prediction-market structure actually behaves, and that information is what a real-time cross-venue terminal exists to surface. The arb is a red herring; the structure is the product.

*An earlier draft of this document attributed observed Polymarket 404 responses on the NYK contract to venue-level liquidity withdrawal. Pre-publication verification revealed that the 404s were caused by a curation error in `markets.yaml` (the Polymarket token IDs were stored as 14-character truncated literals rather than the full 77-character strings). After correction, the NYK Polymarket YES book was queryable and showed lighthouse-mode boundary liquidity rather than full withdrawal. The findings have been revised to reflect the corrected dataset; the README's Errata section documents the full timeline.*
